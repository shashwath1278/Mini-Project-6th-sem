                               ABSTRACT

The escalating global crisis of plastic pollution necessitates the development of innovative
bioremediation strategies. Microbial plastic degradation, facilitated by specialized
enzymes, presents a promising and eco-friendly solution for breaking down synthetic
polymers. However, the traditional experimental discovery of novel plastic-degrading
enzymes is resource-intensive and time-consuming. This project, "PlasticDeg ­ An
Intelligent Microbial Plastic Degradation Potential Prediction System," introduces a
rigorous computational baseline designed to accelerate the discovery of polyester-
degrading enzymes. By leveraging advanced protein language models and highly curated
biological databases, this system aims to accurately predict plastic-degrading capabilities
directly from uncharacterized microbial protein sequences.

The methodology follows a highly structured data processing and machine learning
pipeline to ensure robust and honest evaluation metrics. Ground truth positive sequences
were meticulously curated from the PAZy database and expanded via BLAST against
external libraries under strict 90% identity and query coverage filters. To prevent data
leakage and artificially inflated scores, a homology-aware train-test split was executed
using MMseqs2, ensuring no protein cluster was divided across the training and testing
sets. The predictive architecture utilizes frozen ESM-2 (650M parameter) protein
embeddings coupled with downstream classifiers, specifically Random Forest and Logistic
Regression algorithms. Furthermore, the training pipeline incorporates a tier-weighted
approach to properly balance highly verified "Gold" curated data against homology-
propagated "Silver" data.

Evaluation on a held-out homology test set demonstrated exceptional separability, with
both classifiers achieving near-perfect Precision-Recall and ROC Area Under the Curve
(AUC) scores against broad Swiss-Prot baseline negatives. Specifically, the Random Forest
model exhibited a precision of 1.0 with zero false positives at its training-frozen threshold.
To validate the model's true robustness and ensure it was not simply identifying generic
hydrolases, a rigorous Phase-4 stress test was conducted using reviewed EC 3.1.1 and 3.1.2
Swiss-Prot hydrolases as hard negatives. The system successfully maintained a 0.0 false-
positive rate against these confounding enzymes. These results confirm that PlasticDeg
successfully leverages deep learning representations to isolate plastic-degrading properties
from general protein backgrounds, establishing it as a highly reliable and precise screening
pipeline for novel bioremediation candidates.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. General Introduction

The rapid accumulation of plastic waste has become one of the most pressing
environmental challenges of the 21st century, necessitating innovative solutions for
sustainable degradation. Conventional plastic disposal methods, such as incineration and
landfilling, often result in significant ecological footprints and secondary pollution.
Consequently, there is an urgent scientific focus on biological degradation pathways,
particularly those involving microbial enzymes capable of breaking down complex
synthetic polymers.

The PlasticDeg project aims to address this need by developing an intelligent system for
predicting the plastic-degradation potential of bacteria and fungi using advanced machine
learning techniques. By leveraging state-of-the-art protein language models, specifically
the ESM-2 (Evolutionary Scale Modeling) framework, this project seeks to bridge the gap
between genomic data and functional enzymatic discovery. The system utilizes a curated
database of known plastic-degrading enzymes, primarily sourced from the PAZy database,
to train a robust classification pipeline. This pipeline integrates bioinformatics tools like
BLAST for dataset expansion and MMseqs2 for homology-aware data splitting to ensure
high-quality, non-redundant training. Ultimately, PlasticDeg provides a scalable and
technically rigorous baseline for researchers to identify novel biocatalysts, contributing to
the global effort of mitigating plastic pollution through biotechnology and intelligent
computational screening.

1.2. Problem Statement

Identifying novel plastic-degrading enzymes from vast microbial genomes remains a
significant challenge due to the limitations of current computational models. Existing
predictive methods frequently suffer from artificially inflated performance metrics caused
by sequence redundancy and train-test data leakage, while simultaneously exhibiting high
false-positive rates when attempting to distinguish true polyester-degrading enzymes from
structurally similar, non-degrading general hydrolases. Consequently, there is a critical
need for a rigorous, homology-aware machine learning framework capable of accurately
isolating genuine plastic degradation potential from general enzymatic activity, ensuring
reliable discovery through strict dataset curation and hard-negative stress testing.

1.3. Objectives of the Project

     To construct a high-fidelity foundational dataset: To aggregate and standardize a
     comprehensive dataset of verified microbial plastic-degrading enzymes, creating a
     highly reliable ground truth for predictive modeling.
     To capture deep structural and evolutionary protein features: To leverage
     advanced deep-learning protein representations to accurately map the complex
     biological signatures that dictate plastic degradation potential.
     To engineer a highly generalizable prediction model: To design a robust training
     framework that strictly eliminates sequence redundancy and data leakage, ensuring the
     model's capacity to discover truly novel, uncharacterized enzymes.
     To rigorously validate model specificity: To benchmark the system against
     confounding general enzymes (hard negatives), ensuring the framework explicitly
     targets synthetic polymer degradation with an effectively minimized false-positive
     rate.

     Integrated PlasticDeg Pipeline: A complete Python-based software repository
     containing modules for sequence fetching, embedding generation, model training, and
     rigorous evaluation reports.
     Technical Performance Report: A detailed document containing metrics such as PR-
     AUC, ROC-AUC, and MCC, including an honest assessment of the model's behavior
     at frozen probability thresholds.
     Curated Data Artifacts: A comprehensive set of processed data files, including
     sequence-unique training positives, BLAST expansion logs, and the ESM-2
     embedding caches in .npz format.
     Manuscript & Appendix Bundle: A curated package of discussion snippets, alias
     tables, and evaluation logs ready for academic submission or project presentation.

1.4.1 Deliverable Development and Review Process

The project deliverables were developed using an iterative, pipeline-based methodology to
ensure scientific rigor, data integrity, and reproducibility:

     Data Curation & Processing: Positive sequences were meticulously curated from the
     PAZy database and expanded via BLAST under strict 90% identity and query coverage
     filters. To prevent data leakage, a homology-aware train/test split was enforced using
     MMseqs2

Page | 9  DEPARTMENT OF INFORMATION SCIENCE & ENGINEERING
       PlasticDeg ­ An Intelligent Microbial Plastic Degradation Potential Prediction System

     Feature Extraction & Model Training: High-dimensional embeddings were
     generated using the frozen ESM-2 (650M) protein language model. The classification
     models (Random Forest and Logistic Regression) were trained iteratively using tier-
     weighted strategies to balance Gold and Silver data without artificially inflating scores.
     Review and Testing: Model performance was rigorously reviewed using out-of-
     sample metrics such as PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, and MCC. The system underwent a
     strict Phase-4 stress test against hard negatives (EC 3.1.1/3.1.2 hydrolases) at frozen
     probability thresholds to guarantee the classifier's specificity for plastic degradation.
     Finally, data artifacts and evaluation logs were programmatically validated and
     bundled (manuscript_bundle.py) to ensure an honest and accurate final report.

1.5. Current Scope

     Targeted Enzyme Classes: The current system specifically focuses on polyester-
     degrading potential, using PAZy-centered data as the primary ground truth for the
     "Gold" tier.
     Machine Learning Architecture: The scope is limited to utilizing frozen ESM-2 (t33,
     650M) embeddings paired with traditional Scikit-learn heads like Random Forest and
     Logistic Regression for classification.
     Bioinformatics Integration: Implementation includes local BLAST expansion and
     MMseqs2 clustering to maintain high data quality and prevent homology-based
     leakage.
     Negative Sample Comparison: The system currently evaluates performance against a
     backdrop of Swiss-Prot easy negatives and a specific subset of EC 3.1.1/3.1.2
     hydrolase hard negatives.

1.6. Future Scope

     Expansion to Non-Polyester Plastics: Future iterations can expand the "Gold"
     training pool to include enzymes targeting other polymers like polyethylene (PE) or
     polystyrene (PS) as more verified data becomes available.
     Fine-Tuning ESM-2 Layers: Moving beyond frozen embeddings, the system could be
     upgraded to fine-tune the ESM-2 model layers specifically for enzymatic activity
     prediction to improve sensitivity.
     Web-Based Prediction Interface: Development of a user-friendly web portal or API
     where researchers can upload unknown sequences to receive a real-time "PlasticDeg
     Score" and structural analysis.

2. PROJECT ORGANIZATION

2.1. Software Process Models

For the PlasticDeg project, an Iterative and Incremental Development Model was adopted
to handle the complexities of integrating bioinformatics data with machine learning.

     Modular Development Phase: The system was broken down into independent
     modules (e.g., fetch_sequences.py, embed_sequences.py) to allow for isolated testing
     of the bioinformatics pipeline before moving to the ML phase.
     Versioned Refinement (v1 to v2): The project utilized versioned increments, such as
     moving from the baseline train_esm_baseline.py to a more complex
     train_esm_baseline_v2.py that introduced tier-weighted training.
     Continuous Integration of Data: New "Silver" tier data from BLAST expansions
     were integrated iteratively, followed by a re-run of the manuscript_bundle.py to refresh
     all performance metrics automatically.
     Rapid Prototyping: Early versions of the ESM-2 embeddings were tested on "easy
     negatives" to establish a baseline before the more rigorous "Phase-4" hard negative
     stress tests were implemented.

rapid advancements, particularly in the computational discovery of novel enzymes. To
engineer a robust and highly accurate predictive system like PlasticDeg, it is essential to
establish a deep understanding of both the biological mechanisms underlying polymer
degradation and the state-of-the-art computational methodologies used to model them. This
literature review explores the foundational research that informed the architectural and
strategic decisions of this project.

The reviewed literature spans several critical domains: the curation and biochemical
profiling of plastic-degrading enzymes (such as those cataloged in PAZy and PlasticDB),
the application of advanced deep-learning representations like Protein Language Models
(e.g., ESM-2), and the rigorous evaluation frameworks required to prevent data leakage in
bioinformatics machine learning. By critically analyzing prior works, this review identifies
existing limitations in the field--most notably, the frequent failure of predictive models to
distinguish true polyester-degrading enzymes from structurally similar, non-degrading
general hydrolases.

To ensure a comprehensive analysis of the current research landscape, the project team
systematically investigated key peer-reviewed publications encompassing these themes.

3.3 Conclusion of Literature Survey

The literature survey reveals a significant paradigm shift in bioinformatics, moving from
traditional sequence alignment methods toward advanced Deep Learning (DL)
architectures and Protein Language Models (PLMs). Studies leveraging models such as
ProtBERT, ProtT5, and ESM-2 have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in extracting
deep evolutionary and structural features directly from raw amino acid sequences.
Approaches incorporating contrastive learning, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and
ensemble machine learning algorithms (like Random Forest and SVM) have consistently
achieved high predictive accuracy (often exceeding 85-90%) and strong F1-scores in
predicting Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers and identifying potential plastic-degrading
candidates. Furthermore, AI-driven pipelines like PEZY-Miner showcase the viability of
these computational models in drastically accelerating the discovery of novel plastic-
degrading enzymes.

However, the survey also highlights critical limitations within the current research
landscape. Many state-of-the-art transformer models require immense computational
resources and struggle with class imbalances. Models specifically show decreased accuracy
when predicting highly specific hierarchical levels, such as the 4th digit of EC numbers, or
when dealing with rare enzyme classes. Additionally, the literature frequently notes that
existing models often lack rigorous experimental validation and rely heavily on the
availability of well-curated training data, leading to challenges with small or imbalanced
datasets.

Consequently, there is a clear imperative to develop a more rigorous and highly curated
predictive framework. The insights gathered from this literature review directly inform the
architecture of the PlasticDeg system. By addressing the identified gaps--specifically
through the implementation of MMseqs2-based homology splitting to prevent data leakage,
tier-weighted training to manage dataset confidence, and strict Phase-4 stress testing
against hard negatives--PlasticDeg aims to provide a highly reliable, scientifically sound,
and computationally efficient baseline for discovering true microbial plastic degradation
potential.

4.2. Risk Identification

     Homology-Driven Data Leakage: A primary risk is the potential for sequence
     similarity between the training and testing datasets to artificially inflate performance
     metrics. The project mitigates this by using MMseqs2 for homology-aware clustering,
     ensuring that entire protein families are kept within a single split rather than being
     shared across boundaries.
     False Positives from Functional Similarity: There is a technical risk that the model
     might misclassify general hydrolases (specifically EC 3.1.1 and 3.1.2) as plastic-
     degraders due to their structural similarities. To address this, the system includes a
     Phase-4 hard negative stress test to verify the model's specificity.
     "Silver" Tier Data Reliability: Expanding the dataset with homology-propagated
     "Silver" sequences introduces the risk of including biological noise. This is managed by
     applying a lower training weight of 0.5 to Silver sequences, compared to the 1.0 weight
     assigned to "Gold" (PAZy-curated) sequences.
     Metric Saturation on Easy Negatives: The use of broad Swiss-Prot negatives creates a
     risk of over-optimistic results, such as the near-perfect 1.0 ROC-AUC scores observed.
     These metrics primarily prove that ESM-2 embeddings can separate basic distributions
     rather than demonstrating real-world deployment readiness.
     Threshold-Induced Recall Loss: To maintain high precision and zero false positives
     on hard negatives, the model operates at a very high probability threshold (approx. 0.88
     for Random Forest). This presents the risk of a conservative recall rate, where the
     system identifies only about 52.8% of actual positives.

6. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION

6.1 Introduction

System design is a critical phase that bridges the gap between problem formulation and
technical implementation. For the PlasticDeg pipeline, the architecture was engineered to
handle complex biological sequence data while strictly preventing data leakage and
ensuring reproducible machine learning evaluations. This chapter outlines the high-level
structural design, the sequential flow of data through various transformation states, and the
specific technology stack utilized to build the end-to-end predictive framework.

6.2 Architecture Design

The PlasticDeg system employs a highly modular, pipeline-oriented architecture. Instead of
a monolithic application, the system is decoupled into distinct functional stages, allowing
independent execution, auditing, and validation at each step.
The architecture consists of five core layers:

  1.Data Ingestion & Expansion Layer: Extracts ground truth data from the PAZy
     database and uses BLAST against external libraries (PlasticDB) to expand the pool of
     positive sequences.

  2.Integration & Splitting Layer: Deduplicates sequences and enforces an MMseqs2-
     driven homology split to strictly separate training and testing clusters.

  3.Deep Feature Extraction Layer: Passes the raw amino acid sequences through a
     frozen transformer language model to generate high-dimensional embeddings.

  4.Classification & Training Layer: Applies tier-weighted machine learning algorithms
     to learn the boundaries of degradation potential based on the extracted embeddings.

  5.Evaluation & Artifact Layer: Stress-tests the model against hard negatives and
     automatically compiles the out-of-sample metrics into a static manuscript bundle.

AUC metrics.

6.5.1 Dataset Collection and Preparation

The foundational integrity of any machine learning model relies heavily on its training data.
For PlasticDeg, dataset preparation required strict curation to prevent the learning of
redundant or unverified features.

     Primary Curation (The "Gold" Tier): The ground truth positive dataset was
     extracted from the PAZy (Plastics-Active Enzymes) database. These sequences
     represent experimentally verified, high-confidence plastic-degrading enzymes.
     BLAST-Based Expansion (The "Silver" Tier): To increase the model's coverage, the
     Gold sequences were used as queries to search external biological libraries (such as
     PlasticDB) using local BLAST+. To ensure quality, the implementation enforced strict
     algorithmic filters: only hits meeting a minimum of 90% sequence identity and 90%
     query coverage were retained. This expanded the pool to 325 raw sequences.
     Integration and Sequence-Level Deduplication: A critical preparation step was the
     merging of these datasets. Because identical amino acid sequences will yield
     mathematically identical ESM-2 embeddings, retaining duplicate accessions would
     artificially inflate the dataset size without adding predictive value. The implementation
     applied strict sequence-level deduplication, collapsing the raw hits down to 189
     canonical, unique positive sequences for training.
     Negative Sampling: To provide a general background against which the model could
     learn, 1,296 "easy" negative sequences were randomly sampled from the reviewed
     Swiss-Prot database.
     Final Serialization: The fully curated positives and negatives were serialized into
     standardized CSV tables and FASTA formats, stored securely in the data/processed_v2/
     directory to serve as the immutable foundation for the subsequent MMseqs2 clustering
     and ESM-2 feature extraction steps.
(Note: The preparation of "hard negatives" specifically for Phase-4 stress testing is handled
independently during the evaluation phase to maintain absolute separation from the training
data pool).

6.6 Model Implementation

The modeling strategy of the PlasticDeg system decouples feature extraction from
classification. Instead of training a deep learning model from scratch--which requires
massive datasets and extreme computational power--the system leverages transfer learning
from a state-of-the-art Protein Language Model (PLM), followed by robust downstream
machine learning classifiers.

        PlasticDeg ­ An Intelligent Microbial Plastic Degradation Potential Prediction System

6.6.1 Deep Feature Extraction (ESM-2)
Raw amino acid sequences cannot be directly ingested by standard machine learning
algorithms; they must be vectorized. The pipeline utilizes the ESM-2
(esm2_t33_650M_UR50D) transformer model developed by Meta.

     Architecture & Scale: The model contains 650 million parameters and 33 transformer
     layers. It has been pre-trained on millions of UniProt sequences to understand the
     evolutionary and structural syntax of proteins.
     Vectorization Process: The model's weights are "frozen" (not updated during our
     training phase). Each variable-length protein sequence is passed through the
     transformer. The token representations from the final layer (Layer 33) are mean-pooled
     to produce a fixed 1280-dimensional numerical embedding for every sequence. This
     high-dimensional vector mathematically captures the biological properties of the
     enzyme.

6.6.2 Downstream Classifiers (Random Forest & Logistic Regression)
Once the sequences are embedded into the 1280-dimensional space, two Scikit-Learn
baseline classifiers are trained to map these embeddings to a binary label (Plastic-Degrading
vs. Non-Degrading/General Hydrolase).

     Random Forest (RF): An ensemble learning method constructed from multiple
     decision trees. RF was chosen because it effectively handles high-dimensional, non-
     linear feature spaces without requiring extensive scaling.
     Logistic Regression (LR): A linear classifier used as a secondary baseline. It requires
     standardizing (scaling) the 1280 features but provides a highly interpretable benchmark
     for how linearly separable the classes are in the ESM-2 embedding space.

6.6.3 Tier-Weighted Training Strategy (v2 Baseline)
Because the training dataset consists of highly verified PAZy sequences ("Gold") and
homology-propagated BLAST hits ("Silver"), treating them equally could degrade the
model's precision. The implementation uses a tier-weighted strategy:

     Class-balancing weights are first computed automatically by the algorithm to handle the
     size difference between positives (189) and easy negatives (1296).
     Subsequently, explicit sample weights are applied to the positive class: Tier 1 (Gold)
     sequences receive a weight of 1.0, while Tier 2 (Silver) sequences are penalized with a
     weight of 0.5. This forces the model to heavily prioritize the structural patterns of
     experimentally verified enzymes.

        PlasticDeg ­ An Intelligent Microbial Plastic Degradation Potential Prediction System

6.6.4 Probability Threshold Freezing
Rather than evaluating the model at the default 0.5 probability cutoff, the system
dynamically calculates a strict operating threshold during training. The algorithm identifies
the exact probability cutoff required to achieve a >= 0.8 (80%) recall on the training set. For
instance, the Random Forest threshold was frozen at approximately 0.879. This exact frozen
threshold is then strictly applied to the out-of-sample test set and hard negatives to ensure
honest, real-world evaluation metrics.

     information (collapsing 325 raw hits down to 189 unique positive sequences).

     homology_split.py: Interfaces with the MMseqs2 bioinformatics tool (via Windows

     homology. The module then cleanly divides the dataset into training (1155 sequences)

     sequences--to either split. This completely eliminates data leakage, ensuring the model
     cannot "cheat" by memorizing a sequence in the test set that is 99% identical to one in

7. RESULT AND DISCUSSION

7.1 Experimental Setup

The predictive framework was evaluated using a strictly held-out homology test set to
accurately gauge real-world generalization. The total dataset comprising 1,485 unique
sequences was clustered via MMseqs2 and split into a training set (n = 1155; 136 positives,
1019 negatives) and a testing set (n = 330; 53 positives, 277 negatives). All sequences were
embedded into a 1280-dimensional space using the frozen ESM-2 (650M) transformer
model.
The models (Random Forest and Logistic Regression) were trained using a v2 tier-weighted
strategy (Gold weight 1.0, Silver weight 0.5) applied after standard class balancing.
Crucially, the operating probability thresholds were frozen dynamically during training to
achieve a target recall greater then 80% on the train set (Random Forest threshold: 0.8799;
Logistic Regression threshold: 0.9998). All subsequent test evaluations utilized these frozen
thresholds to prevent test-set tuning.

7.2 Evaluation Metrics

To ensure a robust assessment of model performance, especially given the class imbalance
between rare plastic-degrading enzymes and abundant general proteins, the following
metrics were prioritized:

     Precision and Recall: Calculated at the frozen threshold to determine the exact rate of
     false positives and false negatives.

Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC): A highly reliable statistical rate that
produces a high score only if the prediction obtained good results in all four confusion
matrix categories.

PR-AUC and ROC-AUC: Area under the Precision-Recall and Receiver Operating
Characteristic curves, evaluating threshold-independent model separability.

Despite the strong predictive capabilities and the successful Phase-4 stress test against hard
negatives, the framework exhibits inherent limitations typical of deep transfer-learning
architectures. Specifically, the system faces a severe biological interpretability challenge.
While ensemble models like Random Forest inherently calculate and rank feature
importance to explain their decision-making process, the input features in this pipeline are
highly abstract. Because the inputs are 1280-dimensional latent tensors extracted from the
33rd layer of a transformer model, the "most important" numerical features driving the
classification cannot be easily reverse-engineered into physical biochemistry.

9. CONCLUSION & FUTURE WORK

9.1 Introduction

The rapid accumulation of synthetic plastics in global ecosystems demands scalable and
innovative bioremediation solutions. The "PlasticDeg" project successfully addresses a
critical bottleneck in this domain by establishing a highly rigorous, computationally honest
baseline for discovering novel microbial plastic-degrading enzymes.

By prioritizing data integrity above artificially inflated metrics, the developed pipeline
effectively mitigates the common pitfalls of bioinformatics machine learning. The
implementation of an MMseqs2-driven homology split successfully eradicated train-test
data leakage, while the integration of sequence-level deduplication ensured the model
learned from unique biological information rather than redundant accessions. Furthermore,
the application of a tier-weighted training strategy effectively balanced highly curated
"Gold" PAZy data against homology-propagated "Silver" sequences, yielding a highly
precise Random Forest classifier.

The evaluation results unequivocally demonstrate the representational power of the ESM-2
(650M) transformer model. By mapping sequences into a 1280-dimensional latent space,
the model achieved perfect precision (1.0) and a zero false-positive rate against general
Swiss-Prot negatives at its frozen operating threshold. Crucially, the system passed the
Phase-4 hard negative stress test, proving that it does not blindly flag generic EC 3.1.1/3.1.2
hydrolases as polyester-degrading enzymes. Ultimately, PlasticDeg proves that deep transfer
learning, when bounded by strict biological evaluation frameworks, is a highly reliable tool
for triaging vast microbial genomes and isolating genuine plastic-degrading candidates.

9.2 Future Work

While the PlasticDeg baseline achieves exceptional out-of-sample separability, the
intersection of AI and structural biology provides several avenues for future enhancement:

  1.Integration of 3D Structural Embeddings: The current pipeline relies exclusively on
     1D amino acid sequences via the ESM-2 model. Future iterations should incorporate 3D
     structural predictions--utilizing tools like AlphaFold2 or ESMFold. Coupling sequence
     embeddings with structural coordinates (e.g., binding pocket topology) could
     significantly improve the model's recall and its ability to detect remote functional
     similarities that fall below standard sequence-identity thresholds.

     PlasticDeg ­ An Intelligent Microbial Plastic Degradation Potential Prediction System

2.Wet-Lab Experimental Validation: Computational predictions inherently serve as a
  screening mechanism. The most immediate future requirement is to synthesize the
  highest-confidence predictions--specifically those flagged from uncharacterized
  metagenomic data--and subject them to in vitro enzymatic assays. Real-world
  validation of these candidates will provide critical feedback to refine the machine
  learning baseline.

3.Enhancing Biological Interpretability (XAI): The "black box" nature of deep
  transformer embeddings limits biochemical interpretability. Future work should focus
  on Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as applying attention-mapping algorithms to
  the transformer heads. This would allow researchers to trace the model's predictions
  back to specific amino acid residues, potentially highlighting novel catalytic triads or
  mutational hotspots for directed evolution.

4.Continuous Metagenomic Pipeline Expansion: As the plastisphere expands, newly
  sequenced microbial genomes are constantly deposited into public databases. The
  current architecture's incremental .npz caching system is designed to scale. Future
  updates should automate the ingestion of broader metagenomic datasets, utilizing the
  current frozen model in a semi-supervised learning loop to continuously mine for rare
  degradation pathways.
